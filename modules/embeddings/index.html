Distributional semantics

Meaning as Geometry

Explore word embedding geometry, then compare contextual vectors for words in your own sentences.

1 Find neighbors 2 Compare directions 3 Solve an analogy 4 Audit associations 5 Compare words in context
Learning objectives
  • Distinguish the teaching embedding's static vectors from contextual word vectors.
  • Explain why a contextual vector can vary across word occurrences.
  • Use cosine similarity to compare directions.
  • Explain analogy solving as vector arithmetic.
  • Recognize projections and associations as model-dependent evidence.

Does every semantic relation become a clean vector offset?

No. Results depend on the corpus, training objective, vocabulary, and whether a relation is represented consistently. Famous analogies are selected successes, not a guarantee about language.

Step 4

Audit an association axis

Compare profession vectors with two anchors. The score reflects this embedding’s geometry—not a property of the people or occupations.

Observation

An embedding can preserve regularities and stereotypes found in its training contexts.

Intervention

Removing one direction changes these scores but may damage useful relations or leave other associations intact.

Responsibility

Audit the data, representation, task, subgroups, and consequences—not only one diagnostic number.

Exit question

What does a nearest neighbor or association score justify?

State one valid interpretation and one conclusion that would go beyond the evidence in this teaching embedding.

Step 5

Compare words in context

Write two sentences, select one word in each, and compare the vectors produced for those occurrences.

Real model: this activity runs DistilBERT, a compact BERT-family model, in your browser. The first comparison downloads the model (about 68 MB); later comparisons reuse it. English sentences work best.

This score compares the selected word vectors. It is not the similarity of the whole sentences or a percentage of shared meaning.
